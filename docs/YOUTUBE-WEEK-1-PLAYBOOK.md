**One-line promise:** *Operator-grade walkthroughs of the enterprise IT decisions nobody trains you for — SAP cutovers, vendor renewals, master data, AI governance — from someone who sat on the vendor side and the buyer side of the same deals.*

**The target viewer.** The person in the operator's seat, or training for it:

**The niche / wedge.** Almost all enterprise-IT YouTube is one of two things: vendor/consultant marketing (polished, abstract, ends in a sales demo) or certification cramming (PMP/PRINCE2 exam content). The wedge is **"the third seat"** — content written by someone who has been the vendor *and* the buyer, so it teaches the part both sides hide: floor prices, deal registration, what actually fails at 4 AM on cutover weekend, why governance kills more programmes than technology. Concrete artifacts, named T-codes and article numbers, owners and dates — not sentiment dressed as advice.

**Why subscribe (the recurring contract with the viewer):**
- Every video gives you something you can open Monday and use — a checklist row, a sequence, a calculation, a decision gate — not a framework to "think about."
- You learn what the vendor knows and what the consultant won't write down.
- No platform pitch. Nothing here is selling you a tool you have to buy to apply the lesson.
- Evergreen and re-watchable: these are the same decisions every estate faces on a cycle.

**Format & on-camera approach.** Founder on-camera intro + outro (face builds trust and is brand-safe), screen-share for the substance (a spreadsheet, a checklist, a one-pager — the actual artifact being taught). Talking-head-only for shorter "operator principle" videos. 8–16 min for core teaching videos; 5–8 min for principle/explainer videos. Clean, no music-bed clutter — the orange-on-dark "blueprint" brand look from the site (orange `#E67E22`, dark navy, Hanken Grotesk / JetBrains Mono mono-accents) carries to thumbnails and lower-thirds.

**Realistic cadence (start).** **1 long-form video/week, fixed day (e.g., Tuesday).** Add a second slot (a Short or a tight 5-min explainer) only once the first 4 are shipped and the workflow is proven. Better one genuinely useful operator video a week, indefinitely, than three for a month and then silence. Treat each long-form as the source for 2–3 Shorts later.

### Video 2 — SAP / ERP cutover (highest-intent, deadline-driven search)

**Title:** `Why SAP S/4HANA Cutovers Fail at 4 AM (It's Never the Technology)`

**Thumbnail concept:** Dark frame, a clock reading **4:00 AM** in orange. Overlaid mono text: `THE TECH WAS FINE.` Below, struck-through small: ~~migration~~ ~~servers~~ ~~code~~ and one word circled in orange: `GOVERNANCE`. Founder face bottom corner.

**Format:** Talking-head open → screen-share of a 3-control "cutover governance" one-pager (freeze / runsheet / rollback) → close.

**Beat-by-beat outline:**

- **Cold open (on camera):** "I've watched S/4HANA cutovers fail at 4 AM on a Sunday. Several times. And not once did the technology fail. The monitoring was green. What failed was the operating model around it." Establish the seat + the channel promise.
- **Why technical risk is easy and governance risk is invisible:** technical risk is instrumented — dashboards, alerts, error thresholds. Governance risk has no monitor. "There is no alert for *the runsheet owner is working from the wrong version.*"
- **The three governance controls that actually decide a cutover** *(screen-share a one-pager, each control = owner + threshold + decision)*:
  1. **Master data freeze** — it's a control, not a date in a plan. It needs an owner *with authority to stop the migration* when data keeps arriving after the freeze. Name the failure: data lands post-freeze, nobody enforces, the BP conversion error rate climbs.
  2. **The runsheet** — the single artifact that tells 60 people on a 3 AM call what to do next. Version control is the whole game; the classic failure is people working two versions behind.
  3. **Rollback criteria** — a *pre-agreed decision*, not a slide. Named person + specific threshold + time limit, agreed at the steering committee *before* go-live. Contrast: at 4 AM the criteria are met, but the decision is being negotiated live in a Teams call with twelve people, none of whom want to own the call.
- **Concrete validation, not "improve data quality"** *(briefly, to show the "concrete beats abstract" principle):* name real checks operators actually run — BP-MASS validation on Business Partner conversion, MIGO stock-on-hand reconciliation, FBL3N GL line-item validation against legacy balances — each with a threshold, an owner, a sign-off date. The point: if it can't be a checklist row with an owner and a date, it's sentiment.
- **The pre-mortem beats the risk register** *(bridge to the discipline):* a risk register says "data migration could exceed timeline." A pre-mortem, run 90 minutes before go-live, says exactly what's about to happen — names the people, the decision, the moment a different action changes the outcome. One mitigation, one owner, one date per failure category.
- **Why this is deadline-shaped:** SAP ECC mainstream maintenance ends 31 December 2027; transformation capacity is finite, so the practical planning cut-off is well before then. The cutover discipline is the part you can't buy late.
- **Close (on camera):** governance built with the rigour of architecture is what holds at 3 AM. Funnel CTA.

**Description:**
> S/4HANA cutovers rarely fail on technology — they fail on the operating model: an unenforced master data freeze, a runsheet two versions behind, and rollback criteria that were a slide instead of a pre-agreed decision. Here are the three governance controls that decide whether a cutover holds at 4 AM — each with an owner, a threshold, and a decision. Plus why a 90-minute pre-mortem beats the risk register. When something breaks in an enterprise IT estate at the worst possible time, the post-mortem almost always names the wrong cause.

The renewal auto-rolled at +18%, and the report says "pricing pressure." The cutover slipped, and the report says "data quality." The compliance gap surfaced in the audit, and the report says "regulatory complexity." Each of those is true the way "the patient stopped breathing" is true — accurate, and useless. None of them tells you the thing that would have changed the outcome.

Trace any of them back far enough and you arrive at the same place: a decision that had no owner. The renewal nobody diarised because everyone assumed someone else had. The master-data freeze that was written into the plan but enforced by no one with the authority to stop the migration. The rollback criteria that existed on a slide, but with no named person allowed to call it. The failure was not technical, and it was not even, strictly, a process failure. It was an ownership failure. The process existed. Nobody owned the act of running it when it mattered.

I have spent fifteen years in the seat where these costs land — inside global IT distribution, on the vendor side, the distribution side, and the buyer side, sometimes in the same week. The pattern is the most durable thing I have seen in the work. The expensive failures are not the ones where the technology was wrong. They are the ones where the question *"who owns this, and what are they authorised to do when it goes sideways?"* was never answered out loud before the thing went live.

### Ownership is invisible until the moment it is the only thing that matters

Technical risk is well-instrumented. Error rates, latency, failed jobs, reconciliation breaks — they show up in a dashboard, and the dashboard pages someone. The whole discipline of monitoring exists to make technical risk visible before it becomes an incident.

There is no dashboard for "nobody is authorised to call the rollback." There is no alert that fires when the runsheet owner is working from a version two revisions behind, or when the person who set the renewal reminder left the company in March and nobody inherited the calendar. Ownership risk is invisible right up to the moment it becomes the only thing in the room — usually at 4 AM on a Sunday, in a call with twelve people, none of whom wants to be the one who makes the call.

That asymmetry is why ownership gets skipped. It is less exciting than architecture, it produces no demo, and on a green day it appears to cost nothing. So it is the first thing cut when the programme runs over, and the last thing anyone documents. Then one bad night it turns out to have been the most important part of the design.

### Three places it hides

In a **renewal**, ownership hides in the calendar. The vendor knows your renewal date down to the day — it is in their CRM, with a reminder set sixty days out. The buyer who loses money on the renewal rarely loses it in the room; they lose it because nobody owned the notice deadline, so the prep that needed to start ninety days out started two weeks out, after the leverage had already expired. The skill in the negotiation is real, but it is downstream of a single owned date.

In a **cutover**, ownership hides in the freeze and the rollback. The freeze date is a control, and controls without an owner who can enforce them are decoration — data keeps arriving after the cutover date because the person who could say "stop" was never named, or was named but not empowered. The rollback criteria are a pre-agreed decision: a named person, a specific threshold, a time limit. Write them down before you start, or you will be negotiating authority at the exact moment you have no time to negotiate anything.

In an **AI agent programme**, ownership hides in the "after." The pilot team picks the use case, ships it, and disbands. Six months later the agent is producing subtly wrong output as the data and processes underneath it drift, and the first sign of trouble is a user complaint, not a metric — because the agent was launched as a project that ends, when it is in fact a system in production that needs a named owner, monitoring, and a retirement criterion. Most agent programmes that get cancelled do not fail on the model. They fail on the operating model around it — and the centre of that operating model is the question of who owns the consequences.

### Ownership is not the org chart

Here is the trap, because it is the move most organisations make instead of doing the real work: they answer the ownership question with a RACI in a slide deck and call it solved. A name in a cell is not ownership. Ownership is *authority plus accountability plus the bandwidth to act* — the named person can actually stop the migration, actually call the rollback, actually walk into the renewal with leverage, and is the one whose name is on it when the decision goes the wrong way.

A name with accountability but no authority is a scapegoat waiting for a bad night. Authority with no accountability is how you get the steering committee that approves everything and owns nothing. The two have to sit in the same seat, and the seat has to be staffed by someone with the time to actually look. The reason nobody wants the seat is that it is the seat where the consequences land. That is also exactly why it is the seat that has to be filled before go-live, not discovered empty during the incident.

### The discipline is to make ownership explicit, early, and in writing

The fix is unglamorous and it works: every control, every gate, every irreversible decision gets a named owner, an authority, and a date — before the thing starts, not during the incident. The freeze has an owner who can enforce it. The rollback has a person and a threshold. The renewal has a notice date in a calendar and someone who owns the calendar. The agent has a run-and-maintain owner after launch. None of this is new. It is the same rigour you already apply to technical architecture, pointed at the part of the system that has no monitor.

This is the thing fifteen years actually taught me, and it is why Vihren Labs is built the way it is. The standard behind all of them — the seven principles each written as the inverse of a failure mode I have watched repeat — is the closest thing I have to a one-page answer to the question this essay is really about.

### Email 2 — Day 3 · Pure value, no ask (the 90-minute pre-mortem)

**Subject:** The 90-minute meeting worth more than your risk register
**Preview:** Stop listing what could go wrong. Reconstruct what already did.

A risk register lists what *could* go wrong. It's written at the start of a programme, by the people most motivated to underestimate the risks — because they're the same people who need it approved. So it produces slides: "data migration could exceed planned timeline." Accurate. Useless.

A pre-mortem does the opposite, and it's the single highest-leverage 90 minutes you can spend before a go-live. Here's how to run one.

**1. Set the frame.** Gather the people who actually do the work — not just the ones who own the plan. Say: *"It's six months from now. The programme has gone badly wrong. What happened?"* Past tense. The failure already occurred; you're reconstructing it, not predicting it.

**2. Make it specific or throw it out.** "Communication broke down" is a slide. *"The Business Partner conversion error rate was 12% in the dress rehearsal, the basis team flagged it, the PM said it'd be lower in production, nobody escalated, we went live on schedule, and by Tuesday the UK business was running parallel in ECC"* — that's actionable. It names people, decisions, and the exact point a different call would have changed the outcome.

**3. Output: one mitigation, one owner, one date — per failure category.** Not a list of risks. A short list of *owned* actions. The owner is the person with the authority to act, not just the name in the RACI cell.

That last line is the whole game. A pre-mortem without ownership produces a list nobody acts on. A pre-mortem with a named owner and a date per item becomes the most valuable document in the project file.

Run one before your next gate review. It costs 90 minutes and one honest conversation, and it routinely finds the thing the risk register was structurally incapable of seeing.

— Petko

### Email 3 — Day 7 · Soft pitch → IT Vendor & SaaS Procurement TCO ($49)

**Subject:** Why most buyers lose the renewal before the call
**Preview:** It's not the negotiation. It's the ninety days that didn't happen.

A renewal is not adversarial. It's a structured information-asymmetry contest — and the party with better information wins.

The asymmetry is enormous and almost entirely in the vendor's favour. They know their floor price. They know whether the deal is registered and what margin that protects. They know whether it's quarter-end. And they know your renewal date down to the day, because it's in their CRM with a reminder set sixty days out.

Most buyers walk in knowing the price on the last invoice.

Here's the part that surprises people: buyers who lose money on renewals rarely lose it *in the room*. They lose it because they walked in two weeks before the auto-renewal, after the leverage had already expired. The cost isn't bad negotiation — it's the ninety days of prep that never started, because nobody owned the notice date.

You can close most of that gap with a calendar and a model. The discipline is:
- **The notice date is the most important date in the contract.** Diarise it the day you sign. Start prep 90–120 days *before the notice deadline* — not before the renewal date.
- **Right-size to real usage.** Seats provisioned vs active, modules paid-for vs used. That's your most defensible lever — you're not asking a favour, you're correcting to actuals.
- **Build the true three-year TCO.** List price + bolt-ons + overage + the admin time the tool eats. "It feels expensive" is sentiment; a line-item TCO is a position.
- **Log the outcome** so the next cycle starts from a documented baseline, not the last invoice.
