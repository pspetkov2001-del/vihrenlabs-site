## Video 1 — Your SaaS Renewal Is an Information Game — and the Vendor Already Won (Here's How to Even It)

If you manage any software contract, here's the uncomfortable part. By the time you sit down to negotiate the renewal, the vendor has already known your number for months.

Most buyers walk in knowing exactly one thing. The price on the last invoice.

That's not a negotiation. That's an information game where one side has the cards face-up and the other side is guessing. So let me show you how to even it out.

Quick note before we start, and you'll hear a version of this on every video: this is operator education, not consulting, and not legal advice. Views are my own, not any employer's. Confirm everything against your own contracts.

Today: how to close the information gap on a renewal. Three mistakes almost everyone makes — and what good actually looks like instead.

**[BEAT 1 — Mistake one: you start at T-30]**
*Screen-share: a horizontal timeline labelled T-180 … T-90 … T-45 … T-0*
*Lower-third: "Mistake 1 — starting at T-30"*

Mistake one. You start at thirty days out.

At T-minus-30, you are not a competitive threat. You are a retention problem. You're negotiating on their clock, with their pricing logic, against your own deadline. Every bit of urgency in that room is yours, not theirs.

The leverage window is T-90. Ninety days out. And I want to be precise about why it's ninety, not more, not less. T-180 is too early — it's theoretical, nobody on the vendor side moves on a deal that's six months out. T-45 is too late — by then the leverage has already drained out of the room. At T-90 you can credibly signal you're evaluating alternatives, and you still have enough runway to make that signal real.

But here's the date that actually matters, and it's not the renewal date. It's the notice deadline — the day by which you have to tell them you're not auto-renewing. That's buried in your contract, and it's often thirty, sixty, even ninety days before the renewal itself. Find that date. Then count backwards ninety to a hundred and twenty days from it. That backwards count — from the notice deadline, not the renewal date — is where most renewal money is won or lost. And it happens before a single call gets booked.

**[BEAT 2 — Mistake two: you negotiate the price, not the terms]**
*Lower-third: "Mistake 2 — price, not terms"*

Mistake two. You negotiate the price, and you ignore the terms.

A ten percent discount feels like a win. Over three years, on a mid-size contract, maybe that's fifteen thousand euros. Real money. Meanwhile, an uncapped eight-percent-a-year auto-escalation clause on that same contract quietly costs you forty thousand euros and up — and you signed it without a second look, because all your energy went to the headline discount.

The value isn't in the price. It's in the clauses.

*Screen-share: a four-row list, each row revealed in turn*

Row one — the uplift cap. Get it in writing: CPI or five percent, whichever is lower. This is the single highest-value term you can win, because it removes the fight every single year for the life of the contract.

Row two — true-up mechanics. When you go over your licensed usage, are the overages billed at list price, or at your contracted price? If it's list, one over-usage event can wipe out the entire discount you fought for.

Row three — the auto-renewal notice window. Thirty days is normal. Sixty or ninety days is a trap. Miss it by one day and you're locked in for another full term.

Row four — audit rights. Unlimited scope and unlimited frequency is real operational risk. What you want: once per term, thirty days' notice, scoped to the specific product.

Every one of those is worth more over the term than the discount. And every one of them is invisible on the quote.

**[BEAT 3 — Mistake three: you don't know what the channel knows]**
*Lower-third: "Mistake 3 — what the channel knows"*

Mistake three. You don't know what the distribution layer knows.

If your vendor sells through a distributor — and most enterprise hardware and software does — there's a whole layer of information in that channel that never reaches you unless you ask.

There's the floor price, which is usually well below the list price you're quoted. There's MDF — market development funds — which is the money that mysteriously reappears as "I found you another five percent if you commit this quarter." There's the rebate stack, which is the reseller's actual margin. And there's deal registration: if a reseller has registered your opportunity with the vendor, they get to bid at a protected floor that the other resellers literally cannot reach. Which means your nice "competitive" RFP is theatre. The outcome was decided before you sent it.

Here's the thing almost nobody does. You are entitled to call the vendor's account team and ask, directly, whether your account is registered, and to whom. That one question changes what you're actually negotiating. Most buyers never ask it — not because they're not allowed, but because they don't know the question exists.

## Video 2 — Why SAP S/4HANA Cutovers Fail at 4 AM (It's Never the Technology)

**Target length:** 10–13 minutes
**Promise:** S/4HANA cutovers almost never fail on technology — they fail on three governance controls nobody owned, and here's how to build them like architecture.

---

**[COLD OPEN — on camera]**
*Lower-third: "The tech was fine."*

I've watched S/4HANA cutovers fail at four in the morning, on a Sunday. More than once.

And not one of those times did the technology fail. The migration ran. The servers held. The monitoring was green across the board. What failed was the operating model around it — the part with no dashboard.

So let me tell you what actually breaks at 4 AM, because if you're heading toward a cutover, this is the part nobody puts in the project plan.

**[INTRO — on camera]**
*Lower-third: "Petko — Vihren Labs · How enterprise IT actually runs"*

I'm Petko. Fifteen years in enterprise IT — operating, not consulting. This channel is written from what I call the third seat: not the consultant who designed the programme, not the vendor who sold the system, but the operator who's on the call when it goes sideways.

Same disclaimer as always: this is operator education, not consulting and not advice — confirm everything against your own systems and your own programme.

And one timing note, because it's why this matters right now. SAP's mainstream maintenance for ECC ends on the 31st of December 2027. Transformation capacity across the market is finite, so the real planning cut-off is well before that date. The cutover discipline I'm about to walk through is the part you cannot buy late.

Here's the core idea. Technical risk is easy, because it's visible. Governance risk is dangerous, because it's invisible — right up until it's the only thing in the room.

**[BEAT 1 — Why governance risk has no monitor]**
*Lower-third: "Technical risk is instrumented. Governance risk isn't."*

Think about how much instrumentation sits around the technical side of a cutover. Dashboards. Alerts. Error-rate thresholds. Automated tests. When something breaks technically, it shows up somewhere and it pages someone. The entire discipline of monitoring exists to make technical risk visible before it becomes an incident.

Now tell me which dashboard fires when the runsheet owner is working from a version that's two revisions behind. There isn't one. There is no alert for "the freeze was agreed but nobody enforced it." There is no monitor for "nobody in this call is actually authorised to call the rollback."

That asymmetry is the whole problem. Governance risk doesn't show up on a screen. It shows up at 4 AM, in a call with sixty people, when it's already too late to fix cheaply.

So here are the three governance controls that actually decide whether a cutover holds. Each one is an owner, a threshold, and a decision — agreed before go-live.

**[BEAT 2 — Control one: the master data freeze]**
*Screen-share: a one-pager, "Cutover Governance — 3 controls." Control 1 highlighted: Owner / Threshold / Decision*
*Lower-third: "Control 1 — the freeze is a control, not a date"*

Control one. The master data freeze.

The freeze is not a date in the project plan. It's a control. And a control without an owner who can enforce it is just decoration on a slide.

Here's the failure, exactly as it happens. The freeze date passes. Data keeps arriving anyway — a business unit had one more batch, someone needed one more record. Nobody is named to say "stop," or the person who's named doesn't have the authority to actually stop it. So the data keeps landing post-freeze, and your Business Partner conversion error rate starts climbing. By cutover weekend, you're converting against a moving target.

The fix is one sentence: the freeze needs an owner with the authority to halt the migration when data arrives after the line. Not influence. Authority.

**[BEAT 3 — Control two: the runsheet]**
*Screen-share: Control 2 highlighted*
*Lower-third: "Control 2 — version control is the whole game"*

Control two. The runsheet.

The runsheet is the single artifact that tells sixty people, on a 3 AM call, what to do next. It's the script for the entire night.

And the classic failure is almost stupid in how simple it is: the runsheet got updated three times in the final week, and half the people on the call are working from a version that's two revisions behind. So one team is executing step 40 while another team thinks step 40 was deleted yesterday. Now you've got two versions of reality on the same bridge call, at the worst possible hour.

Version control is the entire game here. One owner, one canonical version, one place it lives, and a hard rule that nobody acts off a copy. Boring. And it's the difference between a clean night and a 4 AM argument.

**[BEAT 4 — Control three: rollback criteria]**
*Screen-share: Control 3 highlighted*
*Lower-third: "Control 3 — rollback is a pre-agreed decision"*

Control three, and this is the one that fails hardest. Rollback criteria.

Rollback criteria are a pre-agreed decision. A named person, a specific threshold, and a time limit — agreed at the steering committee before go-live. Not a slide. A decision, made in advance, in writing.

Here's the contrast. The good version: it's 4 AM, a threshold is breached, the named owner looks at the criteria, says "that's a rollback," and the rollback runs. Thirty seconds of decision because the thinking was done weeks ago.

The bad version, the one I've actually watched: it's 4 AM, the criteria are technically met — and the decision is being negotiated live, in a Teams call, with twelve people, none of whom wants to be the one who owns the call. So nobody calls it. You limp forward into Monday on data you don't trust, and the business quietly starts running parallel in the old system. That's a failed cutover that nobody ever formally declared.

Pre-agree it. Name the person. Set the threshold. Set the clock. Before go-live.

**[BEAT 5 — Concrete validation, not "improve data quality"]**
*Lower-third: "If it's not a checklist row with an owner, it's sentiment"*

Quick principle that sits underneath all three. None of this works on vague instructions.

"Improve data quality" is not a control. What an operator actually runs is specific: BP-MASS validation on the Business Partner conversion. MIGO stock-on-hand reconciliation against the legacy balances. FBL3N general-ledger line-item validation against the old ledger. Each one with a threshold, an owner, and a sign-off date.

The test is simple. If a check can't become a checklist row with a name next to it and a date — it isn't a check. It's sentiment dressed up as a plan.

**[BEAT 6 — The pre-mortem beats the risk register]**
*Lower-third: "The pre-mortem beats the risk register"*

One more, and this is what ties it together. Run a pre-mortem, not just a risk register.

A risk register says "data migration could exceed timeline." Accurate. Useless. It's written at the start, by the people most motivated to underestimate the risk, because they're the same people who need it approved.

A pre-mortem flips it. Ninety minutes before go-live, you gather the people who actually do the work and you say: it's Monday, the cutover has failed — what happened? Past tense. And the answers get specific. "The BP error rate was at four percent in the dress rehearsal, the basis team flagged it, the PM said it'd be lower in production, nobody escalated, we went live anyway." That's not a slide. That names the people, the decision, and the exact moment a different call would have changed the outcome.

The output is tight: one mitigation, one owner, one date, per failure category. That single page is the most valuable document in the whole project file.

## Video 3 — Bad Master Data Is Why Your Reports Lie (and Your AI Will Too)

**Target length:** 9–12 minutes
**Promise:** Two companies run the same ERP — one trusts its numbers, one runs everything in parallel spreadsheets — and the difference is the master data underneath, which no AI agent will fix for you.

---

**[COLD OPEN — on camera]**
*Lower-third: "One customer. Three records."*

Two organisations. Same ERP. Same dashboards. Same vendor, same modules, same reports.

One of them trusts its numbers. The other quietly runs everything in parallel spreadsheets, because deep down nobody believes what the system tells them.

The difference is not the tool. They bought the same tool. The difference is the master data sitting underneath it. And here's the part that matters right now: an AI agent does not fix this. An AI agent scales the confusion — fluently, confidently, at speed.

Let me show you why.

**[INTRO — on camera]**
*Lower-third: "Petko — Vihren Labs · How enterprise IT actually runs"*

I'm Petko. Fifteen years in enterprise IT, and these days I lead enterprise master-data transformation — turning manual, messy product and SKU creation into a governed, automated pipeline that actually holds at scale. So this one is close to the day job.

This channel is written from the third seat: the operator who has to make the system work, not the consultant who drew it or the vendor who sold it. And as always — operator education, not consulting, views are my own.

So let's talk about the layer nobody gets excited about, and everyone pays for.

**[BEAT 1 — What master data actually is]**
*Lower-third: "Master data ≠ CRM list hygiene"*

First, what master data actually is — because it gets confused with cleaning up your contact list, and it's much bigger than that.

Master data is the system of record underneath your ERP and your CRM. It's the answer to three deceptively simple questions: who is the product, who is the customer, who is the vendor. Every transaction, every report, every automation references those records.

So when two systems disagree about who the customer is — same company, two different IDs, two slightly different names — every report downstream inherits that disagreement. Your revenue-by-customer report is now wrong, and nobody did anything obviously wrong to make it wrong. The error was baked into the foundation.

**[BEAT 2 — The named failure modes]**
*Lower-third: "Name the failure, don't 'improve quality'"*

Now, the failures. And I'm going to name them specifically, because "improve data quality" is exactly the kind of sentiment that never gets fixed.

Failure one: duplicates with no survivorship rule. The same customer exists three times, and when you try to merge them, nobody has decided which record wins — which address, which payment terms, which ID survives. So nobody merges them, and the duplicates breed.

Failure two: missing mandatory fields. A record is missing the one attribute an automated process needs — a tax classification, a unit of measure — so the automation silently fails or kicks the record to a human. Multiply that by ten thousand records and your "touchless" process is anything but.

Failure three: the same entity spelled three ways across three systems. ERP says one thing, CRM says another, the data warehouse says a third. No single one is "wrong." Together they're unreconcilable.

And failure four, which is really a financial control: bank-detail changes on the vendor master with no out-of-band callback. Someone changes a vendor's bank account, and there's no independent verification before you pay. That's not just a data problem — that's how payment fraud gets through. Create, approve, and pay have to be three separate hands.

**[BEAT 3 — Score it instead of arguing about it]**
*Screen-share: a six-row data-quality scorecard with field-level scores and a defect count*
*Lower-third: "Score it across six dimensions"*

Here's how you get out of the "data quality is bad / no it isn't" argument that goes nowhere. You stop arguing and you score it.

There are six core dimensions of data quality, and you can score each one at the field level.

Completeness — are the mandatory fields populated? Validity — does the value fit the allowed format or list? Consistency — does the same fact agree across systems? Uniqueness — one real-world entity, one record? Accuracy — does the record match reality? And timeliness — is it current, or are you looking at last year's address?

You score those, field by field. You keep a defect log. You keep a remediation tracker. And suddenly "the data is bad" becomes "the vendor master is 71% complete, there are 1,400 duplicate suspects, and bank details are unverified on 12% of active vendors." One of those is a complaint. The other is a position you can act on — and re-measure next quarter to prove you moved it.

**[BEAT 4 — Why this gates AI specifically]**
*Lower-third: "An agent scales whatever the data says"*

Now back to the AI angle, because this is where it gets expensive fast.

An AI agent acts on whatever the data says. It doesn't pause and wonder whether these three customer records are really the same company. It takes the data at face value and it acts — at machine speed, across thousands of records.

So on duplicated or incomplete data, the agent doesn't fail loudly. It confidently produces the wrong ranking, the wrong recommendation, the wrong customer — and it does it in fluent, plausible language that makes it harder to catch, not easier. The bad output looks exactly like good output.

Which means data readiness is a before-you-deploy question. Not an after. You don't clean up the data once the agent is already making decisions on it — by then it's already made thousands of them.

**[BEAT 5 — Governance is the durable fix]**
*Lower-third: "Cleanup decays. Governance holds."*

And one warning, because this is where most master-data efforts quietly die. A one-time cleanup is not the fix.

If you do a big cleanup with no operating model behind it — no data owners, no stewards, no decision rights about who can create or change a record — the quality decays right back within a year. New duplicates appear, new bad records get created, and you're back where you started, having spent the budget.

The durable fix is governance: named owners for each domain, stewards who maintain it, and clear decision rights. The cleanup gets you to a good state. The operating model keeps you there.

## Video 4 — Most AI Agent Projects Fail — and It's Almost Never the Model

**Target length:** 10–13 minutes
**Promise:** Two companies buy the same AI platform — one gets results in a quarter, one cancels the budget in a fortnight — and four of the five reasons have nothing to do with the model.

---

**[COLD OPEN — on camera]**
*Lower-third: "4 of 5 reasons aren't technical."*

Two companies buy the same AI platform. Same models. Same prompts. Same integrations. On paper, identical.

One of them gets real, usable results inside a quarter. The other produces noise, loses the trust of its users in about a fortnight, and cancels the budget.

Same technology. Opposite outcomes. So the variable is almost never the technology. Let me show you what it actually is — because four of the five reasons projects like this fail have nothing to do with the model.

**[INTRO — on camera]**
*Lower-third: "Petko — Vihren Labs · How enterprise IT actually runs"*

I'm Petko, fifteen years in enterprise IT, writing this channel from the third seat — the operator who has to make it work, not the consultant who pitched it or the vendor who sold it. Operator education, not consulting; views are my own.

One honest caveat up front, because I won't hand you a fake number. Gartner has forecast that more than forty percent of agentic-AI projects may be scrapped by the end of 2027. I want to be clear that's a forecast — a projection, not a measured result — so treat it as a signal of direction, not a hard statistic. But it matches what I see: the failure mode is real, and it's almost always the operating model, not the model.

So the first question on any AI agent project isn't "which agent." It's "who owns this, and how do we decide what we build."

**[BEAT 1 — Failure one: no decision rights]**
*Lower-third: "Failure 1 — no decision rights"*

Failure one: no decision rights.

Nobody can clearly say which projects start, which ones stop, and who is accountable when an agent makes a bad call. So everything is technically someone's job and actually nobody's. When the agent does something wrong — and it will — there's no named person who owns the consequence. That vacuum is where trust dies.

**[BEAT 2 — Failure two: no use-case gate]**
*Lower-third: "Failure 2 — no use-case gate"*

Failure two: no use-case gate.

Once the organisation gets excited about agents, every team wants one. And with no objective filter, the programme spreads itself across a dozen weak candidates instead of concentrating on the two strong ones.

Here's the uncomfortable truth underneath that. Most of the use cases people bring you should never have been an agent at all. Most of them should have been a rule, a report, or a better-designed form. A gate forces that conversation early — before you've spent three months building an agent to do a job a dropdown could have done.

**[BEAT 3 — Failure three: broken data underneath]**
*Lower-third: "Failure 3 — the data underneath"*

Failure three: the data underneath is broken. And if you saw the last video, you already know this one.

The agent inherits every duplicate, every missing field, every record that's spelled three ways. It doesn't clean any of it up. It scales it — confidently, at speed. You can have a flawless model sitting on top of a broken vendor master and the output will still be wrong, because the model is faithfully reporting what the data told it.

Data readiness is a before-you-deploy question. Not an after.

**[BEAT 4 — Failure four: runaway cost]**
*Lower-third: "Failure 4 — runaway cost"*

Failure four: runaway cost.

The platform fee is the part everyone sees. It's the part nobody sees that gets you. Token spend that scales with usage. Orchestration costs. The human-in-the-loop review time, which is a real salaried cost. Vendor fees layered on top. Individually small, and they compound quietly.

Without a three-year view of total cost — the same TCO discipline you'd apply to any vendor — the real bill lands after the budget's already been committed. And that's exactly when a programme gets killed: not because it didn't work, but because it cost three times what anyone modelled.

**[BEAT 5 — Failure five: no owner after launch]**
*Lower-third: "Failure 5 — no owner after launch"*

Failure five, and this is the quiet killer: no owner after launch.

The pilot team ships the agent, declares victory, and disbands. But an agent isn't a project that ends. It's a system in production. The data underneath it drifts, the processes around it change, and six months later the agent is subtly, confidently wrong.

And here's the tell: the first signal of trouble isn't a dashboard. It's a user complaint. By the time a human notices, the agent's been quietly wrong for weeks. A production system with no owner and no monitoring is a liability with a countdown on it.

**[BEAT 6 — What the function actually is]**
*Screen-share: a GO / PILOT / PARK use-case gate one-pager, scoring a candidate on task structure, data readiness, value, risk, build effort, with a blocker-rule callout*
*Lower-third: "GO / PILOT / PARK — with a reason"*

So four of those five are governance, and one is data. None of them is the model. Which tells you what the fix is: a thin operating model, built before you scale.

Not a bureaucracy. A thin Center of Excellence. A charter with real decision rights. Eval and quality standards, so you know what "good" means before you ship. A risk register with autonomy tiers — how much is this agent allowed to do on its own. And named run-and-maintain ownership after launch.

At the centre of it is the gate. Every candidate gets scored — on how structured the task is, on data readiness, on value, on risk, on build effort — and it comes back with one of three verdicts: GO, PILOT, or PARK. With a reason attached.

And one critical detail: the gate needs a blocker rule. A fatal weakness — broken data, or a risk you can't contain — has to be able to fail the whole thing, even if everything else scores beautifully. Otherwise a great average hides the one thing that sinks you. The blocker rule stops a good-looking score from carrying a fatal flaw through.

**[BEAT 7 — The blocker rule cuts both ways]**
*Lower-third: "A clear no is enablement. A slow maybe is theatre."*

Now, the trap on the other side. A gate that approves nothing is just as broken as no gate at all.

If the Center of Excellence becomes the place good ideas go to die, people route around it — and you get shadow AI, agents built in the dark with no oversight, which is the exact thing you were trying to prevent.

So good governance is fast and decisive. A clear "no, and here's the reason" is enablement — it frees the team to go do something that'll actually work. A slow "maybe" is theatre. Speed and a reason are the whole point.
